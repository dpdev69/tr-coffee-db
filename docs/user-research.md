# V2 user research

Prepared 2026-09-14 from the owner's V2 brief. **No interviews have been conducted for this document.** All claims below are hypotheses, not findings. Domain checks and repository data audits do not validate consumer demand.

## Decision to resolve

Will enthusiasts repeatedly give us permission to help them discover their next bag through a weekly email and a feed of verified coffee changes? V2 should validate that behavior before accounts, paid consumer subscriptions, or personalized recommendation scores.

## Hypotheses and evidence to seek

| # | Hypothesis from the brief | Evidence from actual behavior | What would weaken it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Enthusiasts check multiple roaster websites to discover coffee. | Reconstruct the last purchase and the sites actually opened. | Most purchases are habitual single-roaster reorders. |
| 2 | New releases are fragmented across roasters. | Specific releases discovered through different channels and how long discovery took. | One existing channel already covers what the buyer needs. |
| 3 | Good or limited coffees can sell quickly. | A specific wanted bag became unavailable before purchase, with approximate timing. | Buyers rarely encounter this or do not mind substituting. |
| 4 | Deals are easy to miss. | A concrete missed offer and how it was discovered afterward. | Price changes seldom affect purchases or are already well covered. |
| 5 | Enthusiasts already rely on newsletters, communities and manual site checking. | The last newsletters opened, community posts used and sites checked. | Claimed enthusiasm does not translate into repeated discovery behavior. |
| 6 | Origin, process and tasting notes affect buying decisions. | Compare the chosen bag with a rejected alternative and the deciding facts. | Brand familiarity, shipping or price consistently override these attributes. |
| 7 | Too much metadata increases decision overload. | An example where comparing labels caused delay or abandonment. | Extra detail consistently helps and is actively consulted. |
| 8 | A recurring discovery feed is more useful than a passive database. | Repeated return to new releases and email-linked visits over several weeks. | Users search only immediately before buying and ignore updates. |
| 9 | Personal recommendations could strengthen retention once taste data exists. | Buyers already maintain notes or repeatedly trust someone who knows their preferences. | Buyers resist recording feedback or prefer unrestricted exploration. |
| 10 | High coffee spend does not imply willingness to pay a consumer subscription. | Existing discovery subscriptions actually paid for, cancelled or declined and why. | Repeated demonstrated payment for comparable discovery value. |

## Interview approach

Recruit an initial 8–12 participants across frequent specialty buyers, occasional buyers and people who recently switched roasters. This is a proposed qualitative sample, not a representative survey or a completed recruitment effort. Do not recruit only friends who already like the project. Obtain permission before taking notes; record participant codes rather than names/emails in project research files.

Use a 20–30 minute conversation. Start without showing the product. Ask for the last concrete example, chronology and alternatives; follow general answers with “When did that last happen?” Do not ask “Would you use our app?” Do not treat politeness, stated willingness to pay, or praise of a mockup as demand.

## Past-behavior interview script

1. “Think about the last bag of coffee you bought. How did you discover it?” Follow the sequence from first discovery to purchase; ask what else was considered.
2. “Which roaster websites or coffee communities do you check regularly?” Follow with the last time each was checked and what triggered that visit.
3. “Have you missed a release or deal you wish you had seen?” Ask for the actual coffee, approximate date and what the buyer did next.
4. “What are the first three things you look at when deciding whether a coffee interests you?” Ask them to use a recent bag or page as an example.
5. “What makes you reject a coffee?” Reconstruct a recent rejected alternative, including shipping and pack size.
6. “Do you regularly buy similar coffees or intentionally explore?” Compare the last three purchases and their reasons.
7. “If one site knew your taste, what would it need to know to make recommendations you actually trust?” Ground this exploratory prompt in a recommendation previously accepted or rejected; it is not evidence of future adoption.
8. “Would you rather receive every new coffee, or only new coffees likely to match you?” Follow with a current newsletter/feed they keep and one they stopped following; treat the preference itself as provisional.
9. “What would make a weekly coffee email useful enough to keep?” Ask which existing email they last opened, clicked and acted on.
10. “What would make you unsubscribe?” Ask about their most recent actual unsubscribe and its cause.

Only after the behavioral interview, show the working feed and ask the participant to find a coffee worth investigating. Observe what they click, which facts they look for, and how they interpret NEW, PRICE_DROP, RESTOCKED and DEAL. Never imply that a badge or recommendation is independently verified when it is not.

## Evidence record

For each participant code, record date, buying frequency segment, last-purchase timeline, channels actually used, deciding/rejecting factors, observed task behavior and counterexamples. Label direct quotations separately from researcher interpretation. Do not save emails, payment/order details, or unrelated personal information in the repository.

After the first round, summarize each hypothesis as unsupported / mixed / supported by qualitative examples, link to anonymized notes, and name counterexamples. Then compare with privacy-safe product evidence: confirmed newsletter signups, outbound clicks, return visits and weekly email clicks. Cohort rates need numerator, denominator and observation window.

## Decision gate

Continue V2 discovery experiments while evidence is mixed. Consider a small optional taste-profile experiment only after repeated discovery usage is observed, users demonstrate a reason to contribute preference/feedback data, and catalog feature provenance is trustworthy. Do not ship match percentages, a paid consumer subscription, or a full account system merely because interviewees say personalization sounds useful.
